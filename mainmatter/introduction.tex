\section*{Context}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{Context}

The logistics industry stands at a decisive turning point. As global supply chains grow more
intricate and competitive pressures intensify, the ability to transform raw operational data
into timely, actionable insight has shifted from a competitive advantage into an operational
necessity. Each day, millions of records are generated across supply chains by multiple
systems, and the spread of digital technologies, IoT devices, and advanced tracking systems has
only compounded the challenge of managing this data~\parencite{kpmg2024}.

Yet despite this abundance, many organisations remain analytically blind --- unable to convert
their own information into decisions that drive profitability. The figures paint a sobering
picture: roughly 89\% of organisations struggle with data integration, and 61\% report that
rapidly growing data volumes limit their ability to exploit their datasets~\parencite{enveyo2025}.
More strikingly, only 7\% of supply chains can currently access live data, analyse it, and act
on it quickly~\parencite{enveyo2025}, leaving the majority of operators reacting to problems rather
than anticipating them. By contrast, companies that have embraced Business Intelligence and
advanced analytics report cutting supply chain costs by up to 10\% and reducing inventory by as
much as 25\%~\parencite{intelligentaudit2024} --- a measure of the potential that remains largely
untapped.

This challenge is especially acute in emerging markets. Algeria's logistics sector is evolving
amid rapid digital transformation and surging e-commerce activity: the number of registered
e-commerce businesses has grown at an average annual rate of 92\% since 2020, and online payment
transactions for goods and services tripled between 2020 and 2024~\parencite{unctad2025}. Market
revenue, at roughly USD~1.3~billion in 2024, is projected to grow at close to 9.6\% a year and to
exceed USD~2~billion by 2029~\parencite{statista2025}, placing mounting pressure on logistics
providers to scale intelligently and cost-efficiently. Yet fragmented distribution systems and
the limited use of technology across the sector breed inefficiency and unreliability --- a
structural bottleneck that hampers its ability to keep pace with demand.

It is within this dual context --- global data complexity and local market dynamism --- that the
present project takes shape. Most supply chain technology environments remain fragmented, with
ERP, TMS, WMS, and planning systems running on different data models, update cycles, and
integration patterns. Even when each system performs as intended, the combined environment often
responds slowly because coordination across systems is limited~\parencite{logisticsviewpoints2026}.
The consequence is clear: decisions are delayed, duplicated, or sub-optimal --- a reality that
erodes both operational efficiency and financial performance.

\section*{Problem Statement}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{Problem Statement}

In response to this landscape, Ourquilane --- a digital services provider based in Algiers ---
has undertaken to equip its partner Yalidine, one of Algeria's leading logistics operators, with
a Business Intelligence platform aligned with international standards. Operating a vast
multi-site logistics network, Yalidine generates massive volumes of heterogeneous data every
day: transport requests, delivery operations, parcel-tracking events, operating expenses, and
workforce records. This information is dispersed across several disconnected systems, making
consolidated analysis cumbersome, slow, and heavily dependent on manual effort.

The central challenge lies not in the absence of data but in its fragmentation and
inaccessibility. Decision-makers cannot readily see how the network is operating, what its
activities truly cost and whether they remain profitable, or how reliably service targets are
being met --- not without slow, labour-intensive extraction and reconciliation work. In a market
where e-commerce volumes are growing exponentially and operating conditions are under constant
pressure, this analytical gap is a serious strategic vulnerability. The question driving this
project is therefore:

\begin{quote}
    \itshape
    How can a unified, automated, and scalable Business Intelligence architecture turn
    fragmented logistics data into a reliable foundation for proactive, data-driven
    decision-making across operations, cost and profitability, and performance?
\end{quote}

\section*{Objectives}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{Objectives}

To address this challenge, this thesis proposes the design and deployment of a comprehensive and
evolvable Business Intelligence system, built on four objectives:

\begin{itemize}
    \item a \textbf{centralised Data Warehouse} that consolidates data from the company's
          heterogeneous source systems into a single, coherent analytical repository;
    \item an \textbf{automated ETL pipeline} that ensures the reliable, repeatable, and
          high-quality flow of data from those sources into the warehouse;
    \item \textbf{interactive dashboards} that turn stored data into clear, decision-oriented
          visual analytics for logistics managers;
    \item a \textbf{proactive alerting mechanism} that surfaces sensitive indicators in real
          time so that deviations are caught early.
\end{itemize}

These capabilities serve business needs prioritised with the MoSCoW method. The primary axis
(\textit{Must have}) is \textbf{on-demand dedicated transport}; the second (\textit{Should have})
is \textbf{parcel delivery}, which tracks the e-commerce express parcel lifecycle at scale. Each
axis is examined along the same three complementary aspects --- \textbf{operations} (volumes and
flows), \textbf{cost and profitability}, and \textbf{performance} (service quality and
reliability) --- so that decision-makers gain a complete view of each activity rather than a
purely financial one.

Methodologically, the work proceeds through an in-depth bibliographic study of Business
Intelligence and dimensional modelling; an analysis of the existing data landscape at Yalidine;
a structured needs analysis using MoSCoW prioritisation; the architectural design of the data
warehouse schema and ETL flows; the implementation and testing of the dashboards and alerting
modules; and, finally, the deployment of an operational, production-ready decision-support
platform.

\section*{Thesis Structure}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{Thesis Structure}

This thesis is organised into two parts, framed by this general introduction and a general
conclusion.

\textbf{Part~I --- State of the Art} establishes the theoretical and technological foundations of
the work. \textbf{Chapter~1} surveys the field of Business Intelligence, covering decision-making
theory, data warehousing, dimensional modelling, ETL design, and dashboards. \textbf{Chapter~2}
examines the logistics domain, presenting key concepts in supply-chain management, transport and
parcel-delivery operations, and the role of analytics in the sector.

\textbf{Part~II --- Contribution} presents the practical realisation of the system.
\textbf{Chapter~3} analyses the existing situation and sets out the requirements and methodology,
including the data-source inventory and the MoSCoW prioritisation. \textbf{Chapter~4} details the
design and architecture of the platform --- the dimensional warehouse model and the ETL,
alerting, and security designs. \textbf{Chapter~5} describes the implementation and deployment of
the backend, warehouse, dashboards, and supporting infrastructure. \textbf{Chapter~6} reports the
testing and validation of the solution and the results obtained against the stated objectives.

The thesis closes with a general conclusion summarising the contributions, discussing
limitations, and outlining directions for future work.
