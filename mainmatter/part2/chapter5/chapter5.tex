\chapter{Implementation and Deployment}

\section*{Introduction}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{Introduction}

Chapter~4 set out \emph{what} LOGIQ is and \emph{how} it is logically structured, deliberately
independent of any particular technology. This chapter is its counterpart: it shows how that blueprint
was \textbf{built and brought into operation}. Each section mirrors a design block of Chapter~4 ---
the warehouse and its pipeline, the backend, the alerting, the dashboard, and the deployment --- now
expressed in concrete technical terms, naming the frameworks and products that were deliberately
deferred from the conceptual design.

The account stays at the level of \emph{general technical aspects}: it explains the significant
implementation choices and how each part of the design was realised, rather than giving an exhaustive
walkthrough of the code; short snippets appear only where they clarify a point. The chapter opens with
the \textbf{technology stack}, which every later section reuses, and then follows the data flow in
turn --- warehouse and ETL, backend, alerting, frontend and dashboard --- before closing on the
\textbf{deployment} that packages the whole and serves it in production.

The \emph{validation} of the platform --- the faithful test environment built to exercise it end to end,
and the results it produced --- is the subject of Chapter~6, and is intentionally kept out of this
chapter, which concerns construction and delivery.

\section{Technology Stack}
\label{sec:ch5-stack}

LOGIQ is built \textbf{entirely on open-source, free software}. This is a deliberate engineering
choice, consistent with the thesis goal of a pragmatic and scalable solution: the whole platform ---
from the database to the dashboard --- carries \emph{no proprietary licensing cost}, can be inspected
and adapted, and runs on commodity infrastructure.

Beyond being open-source, each individual technology was selected against three criteria:
\textbf{stability} (a mature, well-supported, production-proven tool), \textbf{performance} (able to
operate efficiently at national data scale), and \textbf{mastery} (technologies that are already
well understood, which lowers risk and accelerates delivery). Table~\ref{tab:ch5-stack} lists the full stack
grouped by concern, giving for each technology a short description, the reason it was chosen, and its
logo.

Two points are worth underlining. First, \textbf{PostgreSQL alone} backs \emph{both} stores --- the
analytical warehouse and the operational database --- so the operational/analytical separation designed
in Chapter~4 is realised by two databases on one proven engine, not by two different products. Second,
the \textbf{source-system simulation} (FastAPI) is \emph{not} part of the delivered platform: it
reproduces the source APIs only so the rest of the stack can be exercised under realistic conditions,
and it is therefore presented with the test environment in Chapter~6. The sections that follow take each
production component of Table~\ref{tab:ch5-stack} in turn and describe how it was implemented.

\section{Warehouse and ETL Implementation}
\label{sec:ch5-data}

The warehouse and the pipeline that fills it are realised together: the warehouse as a set of tables in
a single \textbf{PostgreSQL} database, and the pipeline as a \textbf{Dagster} project whose assets are
those very tables. This section shows how the layered model of \S\ref{sec:ch4-dw} and the asset-oriented
pipeline of \S\ref{sec:ch4-etl} were built.

% -----------------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{The Warehouse in PostgreSQL}

The four conceptual layers are realised as four groups of tables in one PostgreSQL database
(Table~\ref{tab:ch5-layers}). The decisive implementation choice is the \textbf{aggregate layer}: the
pre-computed summaries are materialised as \emph{indexed summary tables}, rebuilt at the end of each
run, and they are the \emph{only} tables the analytics API reads (\S\ref{sec:ch5-backend}). A dashboard
query therefore scans a small, indexed summary rather than the hundreds of millions of rows in the fact
layer --- which is what keeps responses within seconds at national scale.

\subsection{The Pipeline in Dagster}

The pipeline realises the asset-oriented philosophy of \S\ref{sec:ch4-etl} directly: \textbf{each
warehouse table is a Dagster asset} that declares the assets it depends on. From these declarations
Dagster reconstructs the lineage of the whole warehouse (the asset graph,
Figure~\ref{fig:ch4-etl-lineage}), materialises assets in dependency order, and lets any single asset be
re-materialised on its own. The five stages --- extract, stage, load dimensions, load facts, refresh
aggregates --- are simply layers of this graph. Listing~\ref{lst:ch5-asset} shows the shape of a fact
asset.

% [VISUAL-IMPLEMENT] minimal Dagster asset snippet
\begin{lstlisting}[language=Python, caption={A fact table declared as a Dagster asset: its dependencies are explicit, and the load is incremental and idempotent.}, label={lst:ch5-asset}]
@asset(deps=[stg_parcel_events, dim_date, dim_agence])
def fct_parcel(context):
    # resolve dimension keys, compute measures,
    # then upsert (insert-or-update) so re-runs never duplicate
    upsert_incremental("fct_parcel", since=context.cursor)
\end{lstlisting}

Two engineering properties from the design are visible in that asset. Extraction and loading are
\textbf{incremental} --- the very large parcel feed resumes from the last processed point via a stored
cursor, never a full reload --- and loads are \textbf{idempotent}, using an insert-or-update (upsert)
strategy so a retried or repeated run can never duplicate data.

% -----------------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Scheduling and Observability}

The pipeline runs without manual intervention. A \textbf{full refresh} is scheduled nightly, with a
\textbf{lighter dimension refresh} on a more frequent schedule, so descriptive data stays current
between full runs. Every run also reports on itself: Dagster emits \textbf{lifecycle events} --- start,
success, failure, cancellation --- to the backend, which records each run in its history and forwards
the outcome to the notification machinery, so users learn when fresh data is available or when a refresh
has failed (\S\ref{sec:ch5-alerting}). The pipeline's observability is thus the bridge between the data
layer and the decision-makers who depend on its freshness.

\section{Backend Implementation}
\label{sec:ch5-backend}

The backend is the \textbf{REST API} that mediates between the data stores and the frontend, and hosts
the platform's application logic. It is built on \textbf{Django} and the \textbf{Django REST Framework
(DRF)}: a single, role-aware API over which the dashboard consumes everything it needs --- analytical
data, alerts, and administration --- without ever talking to a database directly.

\subsection{Two-Database Realisation}

The operational/analytical separation designed conceptually in Chapter~4 is realised physically here.
The backend connects to \emph{two} PostgreSQL databases --- an \textbf{operational} store (identity,
roles, notifications, alert rules, ETL run history) and the \textbf{analytical warehouse} --- and a
\textbf{database router} directs each query to the right one: analytics models to the warehouse,
everything else to the operational store (Figure~\ref{fig:ch5-routing}).

The routing is \emph{transparent} to the application code: a view queries an analytics model in the
ordinary way, and the router --- not the developer --- decides which connection serves it
(Listing~\ref{lst:ch5-router}). This keeps the separation an infrastructural property rather than
something each endpoint must remember to enforce.

% [VISUAL-IMPLEMENT] minimal database-router snippet
\begin{lstlisting}[language=Python, caption={The database router that sends analytics models to the warehouse and all other models to the operational store.}, label={lst:ch5-router}]
class AnalyticsRouter:
    def db_for_read(self, model, **hints):
        if model._meta.app_label == "analytics":
            return "warehouse"
        return "default"
\end{lstlisting}

% -----------------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Graceful Degradation}

Because the two planes are separate, a brief unavailability of the warehouse need not break the
application. The analytics endpoints are written to \textbf{fail safe}: when the warehouse cannot be
reached, they return a representative response rather than an error, so the frontend falls back to
demonstration figures and the interface stays usable. This realises the graceful-degradation requirement
(TS08) and proved useful both operationally and when demonstrating the platform without a live
warehouse.

\section{Alerting Implementation}
\label{sec:ch5-alerting}

The proactive alerting designed in \S\ref{sec:ch4-app} is realised as a \textbf{background service}
built on \textbf{Celery} with \textbf{Redis} as its message broker. Keeping evaluation off the request
path is the point: rules are checked by a worker on a schedule, so watching the indicators never slows
the interface a decision-maker is using.

% -----------------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Scheduled Rule Evaluation}

A periodic Celery task wakes on a fixed schedule and evaluates every \emph{active} rule
(Listing~\ref{lst:ch5-alert}). For each rule it reads the current value of the metric from the warehouse
aggregate layer, compares it against the rule's operator and threshold, and --- if the threshold is
crossed \emph{and} the cooldown has elapsed --- raises an alert and resets the cooldown. This is the
happy path of the alerting flow already shown conceptually in Figure~\ref{fig:ch4-alert-flow}; the
cooldown is what prevents a still-breached metric from alerting on every cycle.

% [VISUAL-IMPLEMENT] minimal Celery rule-evaluation snippet
\begin{lstlisting}[language=Python, caption={The scheduled task that evaluates active rules and fans out an alert when one is breached.}, label={lst:ch5-alert}]
@shared_task
def evaluate_rules():
    for rule in AlertRule.objects.filter(active=True):
        value = read_metric(rule.metric)        # warehouse aggregate
        if rule.is_breached(value) and rule.cooldown_elapsed():
            fan_out(rule.raise_alert(value))     # deliver per user prefs
\end{lstlisting}

% -----------------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Notification Machinery and Channels}

Once raised, an alert is \textbf{fanned out} to the channels each user has enabled --- pushed to the
interface in real time as an \emph{in-app} notification, sent by \emph{email}, or both. The choice is a
\textbf{per-user preference} held in the operational database, so the same alert reaches different
decision-makers through the channels each prefers. Email and other slow work are themselves handed to
Celery, so delivery never blocks evaluation.

Crucially, this notification machinery is \emph{shared}: the ETL lifecycle events emitted by the
pipeline (\S\ref{sec:ch5-data}) are delivered through exactly the same path, so a user is told both when
a business indicator drifts and when fresh data has arrived or a refresh has failed. One controlled
channel therefore carries both the data's \emph{freshness} and the business's \emph{drift} --- closing
the loop between the pipeline, the warehouse, and the people who act on them.

\section{Frontend and Dashboard Implementation}
\label{sec:ch5-frontend}

The frontend is the interface decision-makers actually use. It is realised as a \textbf{single-page
application} built with \textbf{Next.js} (React, TypeScript): a type-safe, component-based UI that
consumes the backend API and never touches a database directly. This section describes how the dashboard
of \S\ref{sec:ch4-app} was built --- its structure, its visualisations, and its interactivity.

% -----------------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Structure and Role-Filtered Navigation}

The interface is organised exactly as the design prescribed (Table~\ref{tab:ch4-app-nav}): a
consolidated \textbf{Overview}, one page per \textbf{business axis} --- Parcel Delivery and Dedicated
Transport --- each with its three views (Operations, Cost \& Profitability, Performance), plus
\textbf{Alerts}, \textbf{Settings}, and \textbf{Administration}. Navigation is \textbf{role-filtered}:
on sign-in the frontend reads the user's permissions from the backend and renders only the sections
their role allows, so the menu itself reflects the role-based access control of
\S\ref{sec:ch5-backend}.

\subsection{Interactivity}

Three interactions make the dashboard a tool to explore rather than just read. A \textbf{global filter
bar} --- period, service type, region, agency --- is shared across a page, so adjusting it re-synchronises
\emph{every} visualisation on that page at once. A configured filter set can be saved as a named
\textbf{snapshot (bookmark)}, letting a decision-maker return to a recurring analysis in one click.
Finally, the \textbf{real-time notifications} raised by the alerting service (\S\ref{sec:ch5-alerting})
appear directly in the interface, so alerts and fresh-data signals reach the user without a page
refresh.

% -----------------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Containerisation and Persistence}

The stack is described by a single \textbf{Docker Compose} orchestration definition that declares every
container, its configuration, and how the containers are wired together --- so the platform can be
started, stopped, or rebuilt as one unit. State that must outlive a rebuild --- the two databases and
the pipeline-run history --- is held on \textbf{named volumes}, so redeploying the application never
loses data.

% -----------------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Reverse Proxy, TLS, and Network Isolation}

An \textbf{Nginx} reverse proxy fronts the stack: it routes public sub-domains to the appropriate
internal container and terminates \textbf{HTTPS}, with certificates issued and \emph{automatically
renewed} by \textbf{Let's Encrypt}. The security posture is deliberately tight --- \emph{only} the
proxy's web port is exposed to the outside, while every other container (databases, ETL, worker, and
even the application servers) sits on a \textbf{private internal network}, unreachable directly from the
internet. This realises the encrypted-transport and isolation requirements of \S\ref{sec:ch4-security}
(TS11).

% -----------------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Topology and Automation}

The complete stack is designed to run on a \textbf{single modest virtual private server} ---
demonstrating that a full Business Intelligence platform can be delivered cost-effectively, without a
fleet of machines (TS19). Once deployed, it runs \textbf{without routine manual intervention}: the ETL
executes on its schedule (\S\ref{sec:ch5-data}), the TLS certificates renew themselves, and the
containers restart automatically on failure or reboot. The platform therefore not only \emph{works} but
keeps working, which is what makes it a deliverable system rather than a prototype.

\section*{Conclusion}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{Conclusion}

This chapter turned the conceptual blueprint of Chapter~4 into a working system. Building on a stack of
\textbf{open-source, free} components, it realised each design block in turn: a \textbf{warehouse} of
four layers in PostgreSQL fed by a \textbf{Dagster} pipeline whose assets are the warehouse tables; a
\textbf{backend} on Django and DRF that routes analytics to the warehouse and everything else to the
operational store; a \textbf{Celery} alerting service that evaluates rules off the request path and fans
notifications out to each user's chosen channels; a \textbf{Next.js} dashboard organised by the two axes
and their three views; and a fully \textbf{containerised} deployment that runs the whole stack behind a
reverse proxy on a single, modest server.

Two threads run through the realisation. It is \emph{cost-effective and reproducible} --- no licensing
cost, one orchestration definition, one-command bring-up on commodity infrastructure --- and it is
\emph{automated}: the pipeline refreshes on schedule, certificates renew themselves, and the platform
keeps working without routine intervention. The blueprint is now a deliverable system.

What remains is to \emph{show that it works}. Because the real production systems could not be connected
during development, the platform was exercised end to end against a faithful test environment, and the
results that justify the project were measured against it --- the subject of Chapter~6.
