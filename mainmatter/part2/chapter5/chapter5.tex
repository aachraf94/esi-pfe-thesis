\chapter{Implementation and Deployment}

\section*{Introduction}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{Introduction}

Chapter~4 set out \emph{what} LOGIQ is and \emph{how} it is logically structured, deliberately
independent of any particular technology. This chapter is its counterpart: it shows how that blueprint
was \textbf{built and brought into operation}. Each section mirrors a design block of Chapter~4 ---
the warehouse and its pipeline, the backend, the dashboard, the alerting, and the deployment --- now
expressed in concrete technical terms, naming the frameworks and products that were deliberately
deferred from the conceptual design.

The account stays at the level of \emph{general technical aspects}: it explains the significant
implementation choices and how each part of the design was realised, rather than giving an exhaustive
walkthrough of the code; short snippets appear only where they clarify a point. The chapter opens with
the \textbf{technology stack}, which every later section reuses, and then follows the data flow in
turn --- warehouse and ETL, backend, frontend and dashboard, alerting --- before closing on the
\textbf{deployment} that packages the whole and serves it in production.

The \emph{validation} of the platform --- the faithful test environment built to exercise it end to end,
and the results it produced --- is the subject of Chapter~6, and is intentionally kept out of this
chapter, which concerns construction and delivery.

\section{Technology Stack}

LOGIQ is built \textbf{entirely on open-source, free software}. This is a deliberate engineering
choice, consistent with the thesis goal of a pragmatic and scalable solution: the whole platform ---
from the database to the dashboard --- carries \emph{no proprietary licensing cost}, can be inspected
and adapted, and runs on commodity infrastructure.

\textbf{stability} (a mature, well-supported, production-proven tool), \textbf{performance} (able to

well understood, which lowers risk and accelerates delivery). Table~\ref{tab:ch5-stack} lists the full stack

Two points are worth underlining. First, \textbf{PostgreSQL alone} backs \emph{both} stores --- the

\section{Warehouse and ETL Implementation}
\label{sec:ch5-data}

The warehouse and the pipeline that fills it are realised together: the warehouse as a set of tables in
a single \textbf{PostgreSQL} database, and the pipeline as a \textbf{Dagster} project whose assets are
those very tables. This section shows how the layered model of \S\ref{sec:ch4-dw} and the asset-oriented
pipeline of \S\ref{sec:ch4-etl} were built.

% -----------------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{The Warehouse in PostgreSQL}

The warehouse lives in a single PostgreSQL database, organised as the four layers designed in
\S\ref{sec:ch4-dw} (Table~\ref{tab:ch5-layers}). Each layer is a group of objects that builds on the one
before it, so the schema is created in a strict dependency order --- \textbf{staging $\rightarrow$
dimensions $\rightarrow$ facts $\rightarrow$ aggregates} --- by a set of \textbf{ordered, idempotent SQL
scripts}, one group per layer. Because the scripts are idempotent, the whole schema can be (re)built
deterministically from scratch, with no manual step.

The decisive implementation choice is the \textbf{aggregate layer}. Its summaries are realised as
PostgreSQL \textbf{materialised views} --- pre-computed, indexed, and \emph{refreshed} at the end of
every pipeline run --- and they are the \emph{only} objects the analytics API reads
(\S\ref{sec:ch5-backend}). A dashboard query therefore scans a small, indexed view instead of the
tens of millions of rows in the fact layer, which is what keeps responses within seconds at national
scale.

The dimensions carry history where it matters. Slowly changing attributes --- an employee's agency or
post, for instance --- are tracked with \textbf{SCD Type~2}: rather than overwriting a changed value,
the pipeline closes the current row and opens a new one, each row carrying a \textbf{surrogate key} and
a \textbf{validity window} (\texttt{valid\_from} / \texttt{valid\_to}) together with an
\texttt{is\_current} flag. A fact therefore always points to the version of the dimension that was valid
when the event occurred. Figure~\ref{fig:ch5-scd2} shows this on the employee dimension, and the
complete constellation model --- every dimension and fact with its keys --- is given as a full
entity-relationship diagram in Appendix~A.

\subsection{The ETL Pipeline in Dagster}

The pipeline is built with \textbf{Dagster} on an \textbf{asset-oriented} model: every warehouse object
--- each staging table, dimension, fact, and aggregate view --- is declared as an \emph{asset} that
names the assets it depends on. From those declarations Dagster reconstructs the lineage of the whole
warehouse and materialises assets in \textbf{dependency order}, which mirrors the build order of the
schema exactly: \textbf{staging $\rightarrow$ dimensions $\rightarrow$ facts $\rightarrow$ aggregates}
(Figure~\ref{fig:ch5-asset-graph}). Because each asset is addressable on its own, a single table can be
re-materialised without rerunning the entire pipeline.

% [VISUAL-PLACEHOLDER] (provided) Dagster asset graph of the warehouse, organised by layer
\fig[\textwidth]{stg_dim_fact_agg}{The warehouse pipeline as a Dagster asset graph, organised by layer:
the staging assets feed the snowflaked dimensions, which feed the facts, which in turn feed the aggregate
materialised views the dashboards read.}{ch5-asset-graph}

Mapped onto this graph, the pipeline follows the three classic \textbf{ETL} phases --- extraction,
transformation, and loading --- each operating between two of the layers.

\textbf{Extraction} pulls data from the source systems' \textbf{REST APIs} into the staging layer,
depending only on the \emph{published API contract} --- never a source's internal database --- so it
runs unchanged against either the real systems or their faithful simulation (\S\ref{sec:ch6-sources}).
Loading differs by size: most staging tables are small enough to be \textbf{fully refreshed} (truncated
and re-filled) on each run, while the heavy feeds --- above all
\texttt{stg\_\allowbreak yalidine\_\allowbreak parcel\_\allowbreak history}, on the order of tens of millions of rows --- are
loaded \textbf{incrementally}, clearing and re-fetching only a trailing one-week window so late-arriving
updates are captured without reloading the whole history. In all, the pipeline consumes eighteen
endpoints across the five source systems (Table~\ref{tab:ch5-endpoints}), presented in Chapter~4.

\textbf{Transformation} turns the raw staging rows into the dimensional model. Records are cleaned and
standardised, natural keys are resolved to \textbf{surrogate keys}, the snowflaked dimensions are
conformed so that both business axes share the same time, geography, and organisation, and the
SCD~Type~2 dimensions are versioned --- closing and opening rows as attributes change, as described
above. The fact \textbf{measures} are computed at this stage too, so each fact lands analysis-ready.

\textbf{Loading} writes the transformed data into the warehouse in dependency order --- dimensions
first, then the facts that reference them --- and finally \textbf{refreshes the aggregate materialised
views} the API serves. Loads are \textbf{idempotent}: an insert-or-update (upsert) keyed on the business
identifier means a retried or repeated run never duplicates data, and a failed step retries with
exponential backoff before the run is marked failed. Together with incremental extraction, this keeps
even the tens-of-millions-row parcel fact current without a full reload.
Figure~\ref{fig:ch5-lineage} traces one fact --- \texttt{fact\_parcel\_revenue} --- back through the
staging feed and the dimensions it is built from, as Dagster records its lineage.

\subsection{Scheduling and Observability}

The pipeline runs without manual intervention, on a \textbf{daily schedule}: Dagster launches a full
materialisation every day at \textbf{01:00 UTC} (02:00 Algerian time), so each morning's dashboards rest
on data refreshed overnight. Figure~\ref{fig:ch5-automation} shows the schedule and the completion sensor
as configured in Dagster.

% [VISUAL-PLACEHOLDER] (provided) Dagster automation page — daily schedule + completion sensor
\fig[0.95\textwidth]{automation_dagster_scrennshot}{The pipeline's automation in Dagster: the daily
schedule that triggers the materialisation at 01:00~UTC, and the sensor that fires when a run
completes.}{ch5-automation}

Every run also reports on itself. When a materialisation finishes, a Dagster \textbf{sensor} fires and
calls a \textbf{webhook on the backend}, which (i) records the run --- its outcome, timing, and any
failure --- in the ETL run history, and (ii) notifies every user who has enabled ETL notifications that
fresh data is available, or that a refresh has failed. This reuses the same notification machinery as the
business alerts (\S\ref{sec:ch5-alerting}), so a single controlled channel carries both the data's
freshness and the business's drift. The pipeline's observability is thus the bridge between the data
layer and the decision-makers who depend on it.

\section{Backend Implementation}
\label{sec:ch5-backend}

The backend is the \textbf{REST API} that mediates between the two databases and the frontend, hosts the
platform's application logic, and integrates with HR Force for user provisioning. It is built on
\textbf{Django} and the \textbf{Django REST Framework (DRF)}: a single, role-aware API over which the
dashboard consumes everything it needs --- analytical data, alerts, preferences, and administration ---
without ever talking to a database directly.

\subsection{The Two Databases}

The operational/analytical separation designed conceptually in Chapter~4 is realised physically here:
the backend connects to \emph{two} PostgreSQL databases (Figure~\ref{fig:ch5-routing}). The
\textbf{operational database} holds the platform's own data --- user accounts, roles, alert rules, user
preferences, notifications, and the ETL run history --- while the \textbf{analytical database} holds the
read-only aggregate layer the dashboards are built on. A \textbf{database router} sends each query to the
right one: analytics models to the analytical database, and everything else to the operational database.

heavy analytical reads off the operational database also lets the two stores be tuned, and fail,
independently.

\subsection{User Provisioning and HR Force Integration}

User accounts are not created by hand in LOGIQ; they are \textbf{imported from HR Force}, the company's
HR application that already owns every employee's identity. On import each account is created
\textbf{inactive by default} and granted no access: an \textbf{administrator} then activates the accounts
that need the platform and \textbf{assigns each a role}, which determines the dashboard sections it may
reach (its role-based access control).

To stay aligned with HR Force over time, the backend also exposes a \textbf{webhook receiver}. Whenever
an employee is hired, leaves --- through resignation or dismissal --- or has their details changed in HR
Force, HR Force calls this webhook and the backend \textbf{synchronises} the matching account:
provisioning a new one, revoking a departed employee's access, or updating the stored information.
Platform access therefore always reflects the current workforce, with no manual upkeep.

% -----------------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Graceful Degradation}

Because the two planes are separate, a brief unavailability of the analytical database need not break
the application. The analytics endpoints are written to \textbf{fail safe}: when the analytical database
cannot be reached, they return a representative response rather than an error, so the frontend falls back
to demonstration figures and the interface stays usable. This realises the graceful-degradation
requirement (TS08) and proved useful both operationally and when demonstrating the platform without a
live analytical database.

\section{Alerting Implementation}
\label{sec:ch5-alerting}

Alerting and notification are realised together as a \textbf{background service} built on \textbf{Celery}
with \textbf{Redis} as its message broker. Keeping this work off the request path is the point: rules are
evaluated and messages delivered by a worker, so watching the indicators never slows the interface a
decision-maker is using.

% -----------------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{What is Notified}

The service delivers two kinds of notification: \textbf{ETL-run} events --- a refresh has completed or
failed (\S\ref{sec:ch5-data}) --- and business \textbf{alerts}, each carrying a \textbf{severity}:
\emph{info}, \emph{warning}, or \emph{critical}. Both kinds travel through one shared machinery, so a
single controlled channel carries both the data's \emph{freshness} and the business's \emph{drift}.

% -----------------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Alert Rules and Subscriptions}

Alerts originate from \textbf{rules the users define} themselves. A rule pairs a metric with an operator,
a threshold, a severity, and a cooldown; a periodic Celery task reads each active rule's current value
from the aggregate layer (\S\ref{sec:ch5-data}) and --- when the threshold is crossed \emph{and} the
cooldown has elapsed --- raises an alert (the conceptual flow of Figure~\ref{fig:ch4-alert-flow}). The
cooldown prevents a still-breached metric from alerting on every cycle.

Delivery is then governed by \textbf{subscription}: a user receives an alert only for the rules they have
\textbf{subscribed} to, and can \textbf{unsubscribe} at any time. Creating rules and managing these
subscriptions is done from the interface, shown in the frontend walkthrough
(\S\ref{sec:ch5-frontend}, Figure~\ref{fig:ch5-alerts}).

% -----------------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Channels and Delivery}

Each user also chooses \emph{how} they are notified, through a \textbf{per-user channel preference} held
in the operational database --- \textbf{in-app}, \textbf{email}, or both. What a given user actually
receives is therefore the combination of their channel preferences and their rule subscriptions.

\textbf{In-app} notifications are pushed to the interface in \textbf{real time}, appearing in the
notification centre as soon as an alert is raised or a refresh completes (Figure~\ref{fig:ch5-notif}).
\textbf{Email} delivery is handled by Celery so it never blocks evaluation: an ETL-run e-mail is sent
when a run finishes, while alerts are gathered into a \textbf{weekly digest} --- sent every
\textbf{Sunday at 07:00} --- grouping the week's info, warning, and critical alerts into a single message
(Figure~\ref{fig:ch5-emails}).

\subsection{Containerisation and Persistence}

The stack is described by a single \textbf{Docker Compose} orchestration definition that declares every
container --- the two databases, Redis, the backend, the Celery worker, the Dagster orchestrator, and the
frontend --- their configuration, and how they are wired together, so the platform can be started,
stopped, or rebuilt as one unit. The simulated source systems of Chapter~6 run as a \emph{separate}
Compose stack on the same shared network. State that must outlive a rebuild --- the two databases and the
Dagster run history --- is held on \textbf{named volumes}, so redeploying never loses data.

% -----------------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Reverse Proxy, TLS, and Network Isolation}

An \textbf{Nginx} reverse proxy fronts the stack: it routes each public sub-domain to the appropriate
internal container and terminates \textbf{HTTPS}, with a single multi-domain certificate issued and
\emph{automatically renewed} by \textbf{Let's Encrypt}. It also carries the long-lived event stream that
delivers \textbf{real-time notifications} to the interface (\S\ref{sec:ch5-alerting}). The security
posture is deliberately tight: a host firewall leaves \emph{only} the web ports (80/443) open, and every
other container --- the two databases, Redis, the ETL orchestrator, the worker, and even the application
servers --- sits on a \textbf{private internal Docker network}, unreachable directly from the internet.
This realises the encrypted-transport and isolation requirements of \S\ref{sec:ch4-security} (TS11).

% -----------------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Hosting Environment}

For testing and demonstration the platform is hosted on a \textbf{single Hostinger VPS} --- a modest
\emph{KVM~2} instance (2~vCPU, 8~GB RAM, 100~GB disk) running \textbf{Ubuntu~24.04 LTS} --- which alone
runs the entire stack, confirming that a full Business Intelligence platform can be delivered
cost-effectively, without a fleet of machines (TS19). It is served from the domain \texttt{logiq.space},
with one sub-domain per externally reachable service (Table~\ref{tab:ch5-urls});
Figure~\ref{fig:ch5-hostinger} shows the VPS and its running containers in the Hostinger console.

\emph{This Hostinger deployment is temporary} --- it exists only to test and demonstrate the platform.
The \textbf{production deployment will be provisioned, and costed, by the company}.

\section*{Conclusion}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{Conclusion}

This chapter turned the conceptual blueprint of Chapter~4 into a working system. Building on a stack of
\textbf{open-source, free} components, it realised each design block in turn: a \textbf{warehouse} of
four layers in PostgreSQL fed by a \textbf{Dagster} pipeline whose assets are the warehouse tables; a
\textbf{backend} on Django and DRF that routes queries across the operational and analytical databases
and provisions its users from HR Force; a \textbf{Celery} service that evaluates alert rules off the
request path and delivers notifications --- in-app and by e-mail --- to each user's chosen channels; a
multilingual \textbf{Next.js} dashboard organised by the two axes and their three views; and a fully
\textbf{containerised} deployment that runs the whole stack behind a reverse proxy on a single VPS.

Two threads run through the realisation. It is \emph{cost-effective and reproducible} --- no licensing
cost, one orchestration definition, one-command bring-up on commodity infrastructure --- and it is
\emph{automated}: the pipeline refreshes on schedule, certificates renew themselves, and the platform
keeps working without routine intervention. The blueprint is now a deliverable system.

What remains is to \emph{show that it works}. Because the real production systems could not be connected
during development, the platform was exercised end to end against a faithful test environment, and the
results that justify the project were measured against it --- the subject of Chapter~6.
