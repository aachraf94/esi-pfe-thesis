\section*{Introduction}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{Introduction}

Part~I set out the foundations of this work: how organisations turn data into decisions, and why
logistics --- data-rich and thin-margined --- is a domain in which Business Intelligence delivers
particular value. This chapter opens Part~II by grounding those principles in a concrete setting.
Before any warehouse can be modelled or any dashboard built, the existing situation must first be
understood: who the actors are, which systems already run, how the business operates day to day, and
precisely where the analytical gap lies.

The chapter therefore proceeds as a structured analysis. It introduces the organisations behind the
project, studies the systems and processes already in place, and from that diagnosis derives the
requirements, the prioritised business scope, and the methodology that guide the remainder of the
contribution:

\begin{itemize}[noitemsep]
    \item \textbf{Host and client organisations} (\S\ref{sec:ch3-orgs}) --- Ourquilane, the software
          company carrying the project, and Yalidine, the logistics operator that is its final client,
          together with its national network.
    \item \textbf{Study of the existing systems} (\S\ref{sec:ch3-existing}) --- the services and
          business processes in operation, the source systems that record them, and the analytical gap
          they leave behind.
    \item \textbf{Requirements specification} (\S\ref{sec:ch3-requirements}) --- the functional and
          non-functional requirements derived from that gap.
    \item \textbf{Business scope and prioritisation} (\S\ref{sec:ch3-scope}) --- the two business axes,
          ranked with the MoSCoW method.
    \item \textbf{Development methodology and planning} (\S\ref{sec:ch3-methodology}) --- the iterative
          approach adopted and the project timeline.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Yalidine El Djazair Services — The Client}

Yalidine El Djazair Services is the leader of express delivery in Algeria, operating a nationwide

\subsubsection{The Logistics Network}

At the organisational level, Yalidine is run by a general direction supported by three regional
directions, a network of call centres, and a large vehicle fleet. Physically, parcels move through a
\textbf{three-tier operational structure} (Figure~\ref{fig:ch3-network}):

\begin{itemize}[noitemsep]
    \item \textbf{Hubs (main sorting centres)} : the principal platforms of the national network.
          They receive parcels from many origins, perform the heaviest sorting, and redistribute them
          to the regional centres. Yalidine operates four hubs, each covering a part of the country:
          \begin{itemize}[noitemsep, topsep=2pt]
              \item \textbf{Oued~Smar} (Algiers) in the north;
              \item \textbf{Relizane} in the west;
              \item \textbf{Constantine} in the east;
              \item \textbf{Ghardaïa} in the Great South.
          \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Regional sorting centres} : each manages several delivery stations, receiving
          consolidated parcels from the hubs and organising their distribution to the stations attached
          to it.
    \item \textbf{Delivery stations} : the proximity units responsible for last-mile delivery. Each
          station covers a set of communes and is the single attachment point for its drivers,
          assigning parcels to them for the daily delivery tours. It is at this level that the
          precision of volume forecasting matters most.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Business Processes}

Yalidine runs two distinct business services, each with its own process: 

% =============================================================================
\subsubsection{Express Parcel Delivery}

Express parcel delivery is Yalidine's principal activity: the last-mile, business-to-consumer
delivery of goods ordered online, from the merchant to the end customer. Every parcel follows a
well-defined lifecycle, from the moment a merchant hands it over to its final delivery
(Figure~\ref{fig:ch3-parcel-lifecycle}).

% [VISUAL-FETCH] author-provided parcel lifecycle diagram (PNG in figures/)
\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{Parcel Lifecycle 1.png}
\caption{The parcel lifecycle at Yalidine, from pickup to delivery, with the reverse-logistics
return flow for undeliverable parcels.}
\label{fig:ch3-parcel-lifecycle}
\end{figure}

As Figure~\ref{fig:ch3-parcel-lifecycle} shows, the journey runs through six stages:

\begin{enumerate}[noitemsep, label=\textbf{\arabic*.}]
    \item \textbf{Pickup} : the parcel is collected from the merchant and registered in the system.
    \item \textbf{Shipping} : the parcel is packed and dispatched into the network.
    \item \textbf{Transport} : the parcel travels between hubs and regional centres towards the
          destination region.
    \item \textbf{Reception} : the parcel arrives at the destination station and awaits assignment
          to a driver.
    \item \textbf{Delivery} : the parcel goes out with a driver and is handed to the customer.
    \item \textbf{Return} : any parcel that cannot be delivered is routed back to the merchant
          along the same network (reverse logistics).
\end{enumerate}

Behind this high-level flow, the operational system tracks a finer set of \textbf{statuses}, each
attached to one phase of the journey (Table~\ref{tab:ch3-status}). These statuses are the backbone of
Yalidine's real-time tracking and, later, of the operational indicators the platform reports --- a
parcel's movement between them is precisely what the Operations view measures.

Beyond its route through the network, each parcel carries two commercial attributes that the
analytical platform must later measure: its \textbf{delivery type} and its \textbf{price}.

The \textbf{delivery type} records how the parcel reaches the customer:

\begin{itemize}[noitemsep]
    \item \textbf{Home Delivery (HD)} : the parcel is delivered to the address specified by the
          customer.
    \item \textbf{Stop Desk (SD)} : the customer collects the parcel from a delivery station.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Pricing.}
The \textbf{price} of a parcel is not a single figure but a small set of related tariffs
(Table~\ref{tab:ch3-pricing}). A delivered parcel is billed at one rate and a returned parcel at
another. In parallel, a \emph{theoretical} tariff read from the published pricing grid is compared
with a \emph{real} tariff recomputed from the parcel's measured weight and volume; the gap between
the two is a direct signal of cost and profitability.

\paragraph{Reimbursement.}
Separately, a sender may insure a parcel against loss or damage. For an optional premium of roughly
1\% of the declared value, Yalidine operates a reimbursement scheme: a lost parcel is reimbursed in
full, while a damaged one is reimbursed at 60--80\% of its declared value, depending on the
condition of its packaging.

\subsubsection{On-Demand Dedicated Transport (B2B)}

Where parcel delivery pools thousands of small shipments through a shared network, dedicated
transport serves one client at a time: a company commissions a \textbf{dedicated vehicle} for a
specific trip. The process is therefore organised around a single \emph{request} rather than an
individual parcel, and follows the simple lifecycle shown in Figure~\ref{fig:ch3-transport-flow}.

As Figure~\ref{fig:ch3-transport-flow} shows, a request moves through four stages:

\begin{enumerate}[noitemsep, label=\textbf{\arabic*.}]
    \item \textbf{Transport request} : a client company requests a dedicated trip, specifying the
          cargo and the pickup and delivery points.
    \item \textbf{Agreement} : the trip is quoted and, once the client accepts, confirmed.
    \item \textbf{Pickup} : a vehicle is dispatched from a hub and loads the cargo at the pickup point.
    \item \textbf{Delivery} : the cargo is carried to its destination and handed over, and the
          request is marked completed.
\end{enumerate}

Beyond this flow, each request records the \textbf{vehicle and its crew}, the \textbf{cargo} (type,
weight, volume, and any special handling needs), and the \textbf{routing and performance} of the trip
(distance, duration, delays, and the client's rating). Together these attributes make the service
measurable.

\paragraph{Cost.}
Unlike a parcel, whose price is read off a grid, the cost of a dedicated trip is built up from several
components: a fixed base and a distance supplement, the operational costs of pickup, delivery and
handling, any surcharges (night work, perishable goods, insurance), and the direct travel costs of
fuel and tolls. Comparing this built-up cost with the amount invoiced is the basis of the
profitability analysis for this axis.

\subsection{Identified Weaknesses and the Analytical Gap}

Each of the applications described above does its own job well, but they were never meant to work
together. The business is therefore run from a set of disconnected islands of information: the same
parcel, vehicle, client, or expense is recorded separately in several places, with no single
consolidated view of the activity. This fragmentation undermines the very qualities a decision needs
from its data. \textbf{Coherence} suffers, because the same fact is counted differently from one
application to the next; \textbf{integrity} suffers, because there is no shared reference to reconcile
those versions against; and \textbf{availability} suffers, because obtaining a simple cross-cutting
figure --- the cost of an axis, the performance of a region, the profitability of a service ---
demands slow, manual extraction and reconciliation across systems that do not communicate. By the time
a consolidated picture is assembled, it is often partial and already out of date.

The consequence is a clear analytical gap: top management cannot read the business as a whole, along
its operations, its cost and profitability, and its performance, from a single trustworthy source.
Decisions that should rest on evidence end up resting on intuition, on fragments, or on figures whose
reliability cannot be guaranteed. Closing this gap calls for a reliable, consolidated system that
brings the scattered data together into one coherent and readily available basis --- one that gives the
organisation's decision-makers a faithful, timely view of their activity, so that strategic choices
can genuinely be made on data rather than on impression.
