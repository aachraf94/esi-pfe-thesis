\section*{Introduction}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{Introduction}

Part~I set out the foundations of this work: how organisations turn data into decisions, and why
logistics --- data-rich and thin-margined --- is a domain in which Business Intelligence delivers
particular value. This chapter opens Part~II by grounding those principles in a concrete setting.
Before any warehouse can be modelled or any dashboard built, the existing situation must first be
understood: who the actors are, which systems already run, how the business operates day to day, and
precisely where the analytical gap lies.

The chapter therefore proceeds as a structured analysis. It introduces the organisations behind the
project, studies the systems and processes already in place, and from that diagnosis derives the
requirements, the prioritised business scope, and the methodology that guide the remainder of the
contribution:

\begin{itemize}[noitemsep]
    \item \textbf{Host and client organisations} (\S\ref{sec:ch3-orgs}) --- Ourquilane, the software
          company carrying the project, and Yalidine, the logistics operator that is its final client,
          together with its national network.
    \item \textbf{Study of the existing systems} (\S\ref{sec:ch3-existing}) --- the services and
          business processes in operation, the source systems that record them, and the analytical gap
          they leave behind.
    \item \textbf{Requirements specification} (\S\ref{sec:ch3-requirements}) --- the functional and
          non-functional requirements derived from that gap.
    \item \textbf{Business scope and prioritisation} (\S\ref{sec:ch3-scope}) --- the two business axes,
          ranked with the MoSCoW method.
    \item \textbf{Development methodology and planning} (\S\ref{sec:ch3-methodology}) --- the iterative
          approach adopted and the project timeline.
\end{itemize}

\section{Host and Client Organisations}
\label{sec:ch3-orgs}

This project was conducted as a partnership between two organisations with complementary roles:
\textbf{Ourquilane}, the software company that designed and built the platform, and \textbf{Yalidine},
the logistics operator for which it was built and whose data it analyses. The scale and structure of
Yalidine's operations, in particular, frame every design decision in the chapters that follow, so both
organisations are introduced here before the existing systems are examined.

% -----------------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Ourquilane — The Project Carrier}

Ourquilane is a dynamic Algerian startup based in Hydra, Algiers, specialising in innovative software
solutions. Its mission is to modernise a range of sectors through digital platforms, digital marketing,
and decision-support tools, supported by a portfolio of business applications (HR~Force, FLEET~GO,
CASH~Box, Route~Planner, \etc). It is the host organisation carrying the present project; its logo is

\subsection{Yalidine El Djazair Services — The Client}

Yalidine El Djazair Services is the leader of express delivery in Algeria, operating a nationwide
logistics network (Figure~\ref{fig:ch3-yalidine-logo}). Its activity covers B2B courier services, B2C
e-commerce delivery, and cash-on-delivery (COD) collection, and it is widely recognised for the
real-time parcel tracking that secures and accelerates its shipments. Its operational maturity is
reflected in three ISO certifications --- ISO~9001 (Quality), ISO~14001 (Environment), and ISO~27001
(Information Security) --- a standard of rigour that the analytical platform serving it is expected to
match.

\subsubsection{The Logistics Network}

At the organisational level, Yalidine is run by a general direction supported by three regional
directions, a network of call centres, and a large vehicle fleet. Physically, parcels move through a
\textbf{three-tier operational structure} (Figure~\ref{fig:ch3-network}):

\begin{itemize}[noitemsep]
    \item \textbf{Hubs (main sorting centres)} --- the principal platforms of the national network.
          They receive parcels from many origins, perform the heaviest sorting, and redistribute them
          to the regional centres. Yalidine operates four hubs, each covering a part of the country:
          \begin{itemize}[noitemsep, topsep=2pt]
              \item \textbf{Oued~Smar} (Algiers) --- the north;
              \item \textbf{Relizane} --- the west;
              \item \textbf{Constantine} --- the east;
              \item \textbf{Ghardaïa} --- the Great South.
          \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Regional sorting centres} --- each manages several delivery stations, receiving
          consolidated parcels from the hubs and organising their distribution to the stations attached
          to it.
    \item \textbf{Delivery stations} --- the proximity units responsible for last-mile delivery. Each
          station covers a set of communes and is the single attachment point for its drivers,
          assigning parcels to them for the daily delivery tours. It is at this level that the
          precision of volume forecasting matters most.
\end{itemize}

\section{Study of the Existing Systems}
\label{sec:ch3-existing}

Having introduced the organisations, this section turns to how Yalidine operates day to day and how
that activity is recorded. It first follows the two services as business processes, then inventories
the operational systems that capture their data --- the raw material the platform must later
consolidate.

% -----------------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Business Processes}

\paragraph{Parcel delivery.}
This is Yalidine's principal activity. Every parcel follows a well-defined lifecycle, from the moment a
merchant hands it over to its final delivery (Figure~\ref{fig:ch3-parcel-lifecycle}). After
\textbf{pickup}, the parcel reaches a \textbf{hub}, where it is sorted and routed to the
\textbf{regional centre} responsible for its destination, then to the \textbf{station} that covers the
delivery area. The station assigns it to a driver, who takes it \textbf{out for delivery}; on success
the parcel is marked \textbf{delivered} and, where applicable, the cash-on-delivery amount is
collected. When delivery fails or the recipient refuses the parcel, it enters a \textbf{return} flow
back to the merchant.

Beyond its route through the network, each delivery is characterised by two attributes that shape how it
is priced and recorded. It falls into one of four \textbf{distance zones}, from intra-wilaya to the long
inter-wilaya routes of the south (Table~\ref{tab:ch3-zones}), and is fulfilled through one of two
\textbf{delivery types}: \emph{stop desk} (SD), where the customer collects the parcel from a desk, or
\emph{home delivery} (HD), where it is delivered to the address. Both attributes reappear throughout the
analysis as natural dimensions along which operations, cost, and performance are read.

\paragraph{Parcel pricing.}
The price of a parcel is not a single figure but a small set of related tariffs
(Table~\ref{tab:ch3-pricing}). A delivered parcel is billed at one rate and a returned parcel at
another; in parallel, a \emph{theoretical} tariff taken from the published pricing grid is compared
against a \emph{real} tariff recomputed from the parcel's measured weight and volume, the divergence
between the two being a direct signal for cost and profitability. Finally, where a sender insures a
parcel, Yalidine operates a reimbursement scheme covering loss or damage.
