\section*{Introduction}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{Introduction}

Part~I set out the foundations of this work: how organisations turn data into decisions, and why
logistics --- data-rich and thin-margined --- is a domain in which Business Intelligence delivers
particular value. This chapter opens Part~II by grounding those principles in a concrete setting.
Before any warehouse can be modelled or any dashboard built, the existing situation must first be
understood: who the actors are, which systems already run, how the business operates day to day, and
precisely where the analytical gap lies.

The chapter therefore proceeds as a structured analysis. It introduces the organisations behind the
project, studies the systems and processes already in place, and from that diagnosis derives the
objectives of the target system, the prioritised business scope, and the methodology that guide the
remainder of the contribution:

\begin{itemize}[noitemsep]
    \item \textbf{Host and client organisations} (\S\ref{sec:ch3-orgs}) --- Ourquilane, the software
          company carrying the project, and Yalidine, the logistics operator that is its final client,
          together with its national network.
    \item \textbf{Study of the existing systems} (\S\ref{sec:ch3-existing}) --- the services and
          business processes in operation and the operational applications that record them.
    \item \textbf{Diagnosis of the existing situation} (\S\ref{sec:ch3-diagnosis}) --- the weaknesses
          of the current setup and the analytical gap it leaves behind.
    \item \textbf{Needs-gathering methods} (\S\ref{sec:ch3-methods}) --- the techniques used to
          understand the business and collect its needs.
    \item \textbf{Needs analysis and synthesis} (\S\ref{sec:ch3-objectives}) --- the synthesised needs
          and the objectives of the target system derived from them.
    \item \textbf{Business scope and prioritisation} (\S\ref{sec:ch3-scope}) --- the two business axes,
          ranked with the MoSCoW method.
    \item \textbf{Development methodology and planning} (\S\ref{sec:ch3-methodology}) --- the iterative

\subsubsection{The Logistics Network}

At the organisational level, Yalidine is run by a general direction supported by three regional
directions, a network of call centres, and a large vehicle fleet. Physically, parcels move through a
\textbf{three-tier operational structure} (Figure~\ref{fig:ch3-network}):

\begin{itemize}[noitemsep]
    \item \textbf{Hubs (main sorting centres)} : the principal platforms of the national network.
          They receive parcels from many origins, perform the heaviest sorting, and redistribute them
          to the regional centres. Yalidine operates four hubs, each covering a part of the country:
          \begin{itemize}[noitemsep, topsep=2pt]
              \item \textbf{Oued~Smar} (Algiers) in the north;
              \item \textbf{Relizane} in the west;
              \item \textbf{Constantine} in the east;
              \item \textbf{Ghardaïa} in the Great South.
          \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Regional sorting centres} : each manages several delivery stations, receiving
          consolidated parcels from the hubs and organising their distribution to the stations attached
          to it.
    \item \textbf{Delivery stations} : the proximity units responsible for last-mile delivery. Each
          station covers a set of communes and is the single attachment point for its drivers,
          assigning parcels to them for the daily delivery tours. It is at this level that the
          precision of volume forecasting matters most.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Express Parcel Delivery}

Express parcel delivery is the classic e-commerce parcel-delivery service: a merchant sells goods
online and entrusts Yalidine with carrying each order to the buyer. It is Yalidine's principal
activity --- the last-mile, business-to-consumer delivery of goods ordered online, from the merchant
to the end customer. Every parcel follows a well-defined lifecycle, from the moment a merchant hands
it over to its final delivery (Figure~\ref{fig:ch3-parcel-lifecycle}).

% [VISUAL-FETCH] author-provided parcel lifecycle diagram (PNG in figures/)
\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{Parcel Lifecycle 1.png}
\caption{The parcel lifecycle at Yalidine, from pickup to delivery, with the reverse-logistics
return flow for undeliverable parcels.}
\label{fig:ch3-parcel-lifecycle}
\end{figure}

As Figure~\ref{fig:ch3-parcel-lifecycle} shows, the journey runs through six stages:

\begin{enumerate}[noitemsep, label=\textbf{\arabic*.}]
    \item \textbf{Pickup} : the parcel is collected from the merchant and registered in the system.
    \item \textbf{Shipping} : the parcel is packed and dispatched into the network.
    \item \textbf{Transport} : the parcel travels between hubs and regional centres towards the
          destination region.
    \item \textbf{Reception} : the parcel arrives at the destination station and awaits assignment
          to a driver.
    \item \textbf{Delivery} : the parcel goes out with a driver and is handed to the customer.
    \item \textbf{Return} : any parcel that cannot be delivered is routed back to the merchant
          along the same network (reverse logistics).
\end{enumerate}

Behind this high-level flow, the operational system tracks a finer set of \textbf{statuses}, each
attached to one phase of the journey (Table~\ref{tab:ch3-status}). These statuses are the backbone of
Yalidine's real-time tracking and, later, of the operational indicators the platform reports --- a
parcel's movement between them is precisely what the Operations view measures.

\paragraph{Reimbursement.}
Separately, a sender may insure a parcel against loss or damage through an optional \emph{parcel
insurance} (\emph{assurance colis}) set at roughly 1\% of the parcel's declared value. When this
insurance is taken, Yalidine operates a reimbursement scheme: a lost parcel is reimbursed in full,
while a damaged one is reimbursed at 60--80\% of its declared value, depending on the condition of
its packaging.

As Figure~\ref{fig:ch3-transport-flow} shows, a request moves through four stages:

\begin{enumerate}[noitemsep, label=\textbf{\arabic*.}]
    \item \textbf{Transport request} : a client company requests a dedicated trip, specifying the
          cargo and the pickup and delivery points.
    \item \textbf{Agreement} : the trip is quoted and, once the client accepts, confirmed.
    \item \textbf{Pickup} : a vehicle is dispatched from a hub and loads the cargo at the pickup point.
    \item \textbf{Delivery} : the cargo is carried to its destination and handed over, and the
          request is marked completed.
\end{enumerate}

Beyond this flow, each request records the \textbf{vehicle and its crew}, the \textbf{cargo} (type,
weight, volume, and any special handling needs), and the \textbf{routing and performance} of the trip
(distance, duration, delays, and the client's rating). Together these attributes make the service
measurable.

\paragraph{Cost.}
Unlike a parcel, whose price is read off a grid, the cost of a dedicated trip is built up from several
components: a fixed base and a distance supplement, the operational costs of pickup, delivery and
handling, any surcharges (night work, perishable goods, insurance), and the direct travel costs of
fuel and tolls. Comparing this built-up cost with the amount invoiced is the basis of the
profitability analysis for this axis.

\section{Diagnosis of the Existing Situation}
\label{sec:ch3-diagnosis}

The previous section showed that each of Yalidine's applications does its own job well, yet the set
was never designed to work as a whole. Seen from the standpoint of a decision-maker --- rather than
that of any single application --- the existing situation reveals a recurring pattern: the information
needed to steer the business does exist, but it is dispersed, inconsistent, and hard to turn into a
clear picture. This section gathers that observation into a structured diagnosis.

The aim is not to fault any one application, each of which serves its own purpose correctly. It is to
show what is missing \emph{between} them: a system that brings the activity together into one
reliable, decision-oriented view. The weaknesses set out below all stem from that single absence, and
together they explain why, today, management cannot steer the business on solid figures.

\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{\arabic*.}]
    \item \textbf{Fragmented information} : the activity is spread across disconnected applications,
          each holding only its own slice of the business. There is no single place where the company
          can be seen as a whole, so forming any overall picture means piecing fragments together by
          hand.
    \item \textbf{Duplicated and inconsistent records} : the same parcel, client, vehicle, or expense
          is recorded in several applications at once. The same
          question can therefore return different answers depending on which application is consulted.
    \item \textbf{Doubtful reliability of the figures} : without one authoritative source, errors,
          gaps, and out-of-date entries pass unnoticed and cannot be reconciled. Confidence in the
          numbers erodes, and time is lost arguing over which version is correct instead of acting
          on it.
    \item \textbf{Poor availability of information} : obtaining a simple cross-cutting figure --- the
          cost of an axis, the performance of a region, the profitability of a service --- calls for
          slow manual extraction and reconciliation. By the time the answer is ready, it is often
          already out of date.
    \item \textbf{Manual, time-consuming reporting} : reports are assembled by hand, typically in
          spreadsheets. This monopolises staff time, is easy to get wrong, and must be redone from
          scratch each time the question changes.
    \item \textbf{Reactive rather than proactive management} : problems --- a rise in returns, a cost
          overrun, a drop in on-time delivery --- come to light only after the fact, once they appear
          in a periodic report, rather than while they can still be corrected.
    \item \textbf{No consolidated reading of each activity} : management cannot follow either business
          axis along the three angles that matter to it --- its operations, its cost and
          profitability, and its performance --- from one coherent source.
    \item \textbf{Blurred financial perimeters} : the costs and revenues of the two services are not
          cleanly separated, which makes the true profitability of each activity hard to establish.
    \item \textbf{Limited historical perspective} : the applications are built to run the business day
          to day, not to study it over time. Comparing one period with another, or spotting seasonal
          trends, is consequently difficult.
    \item \textbf{Dependence on individual knowledge} : because much of the overall picture lives in
          people's heads and personal files, insight is lost whenever a key person is unavailable, and
          it is hard to share consistently across the organisation.
    \item \textbf{Decisions taken on intuition} : taken together, these weaknesses force management to
          decide on partial, late, or unreliable information --- that is, on experience and intuition
          rather than on evidence.
\end{enumerate}

This diagnosis defines precisely what the existing situation lacks and what a future system must put
right. Translating that into a concrete set of needs began with gathering information directly from
the people who run the business --- the subject of the next section.

\section{Needs Analysis and Synthesis}
\label{sec:ch3-objectives}

The material gathered through the methods above --- the interviews, the management meeting, the field
observation, and the study of documents and applications --- was cross-checked and analysed. Although
the needs were voiced differently by different people, they converged on the same core: the business
must be able to see itself clearly, as a whole, and to act on what it sees. This section synthesises
those needs and turns them into the objectives of the target system, stated in business terms rather
than as a technical specification.

% -----------------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Synthesis of the Needs}

Once analysed, the expressed needs fall into three families (Table~\ref{tab:ch3-needs}):
\emph{informational} needs --- what management must be able to see; \emph{operational} needs --- what
it must be able to do with that information; and \emph{quality} needs --- the conditions under which
the system would actually be adopted.

These needs crystallise into a clear set of objectives for the target system: a single general
objective and the specific objectives that realise it.

% -----------------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{General Objective}

The general objective is to provide Yalidine's management with a single, reliable, and consolidated
decision-support system: one that gathers the data currently scattered across the operational
applications into a faithful and timely view of the business, so that strategic and operational
choices can be made on evidence rather than on intuition.

% -----------------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Specific Objectives}

This general aim breaks down into a set of concrete objectives:

\begin{enumerate}[noitemsep, label=\textbf{\arabic*.}]
    \item \textbf{Consolidate the scattered data} : bring the activity dispersed across the
          operational applications together into one coherent and continuously available source.
    \item \textbf{Read each business axis in depth} : present both axes, parcel delivery and
          dedicated transport, along the three complementary views that matter to management:
          \emph{Operations}, \emph{Cost \& Profitability}, and \emph{Performance}.
    \item \textbf{Keep the two financial perimeters separate} : never mix the costs and revenues of
          the two axes, so that each is read on its own perimeter.
    \item \textbf{Monitor proactively} : signal early, and without manual checking, when a key
          indicator drifts beyond an acceptable level, so that problems are caught before they grow.
    \item \textbf{Deliver the right information to the right decision-maker} : give each manager a
          view suited to their role and responsibility.
    \item \textbf{Found decisions on reliable data} : equip top and operational management with
          clear, decision-oriented indicators that support both day-to-day and strategic choices.
\end{enumerate}

These objectives define what the target system must achieve. Because they cannot all be delivered at
once, the next section sets the scope and the order of priority across the two business axes.

\section{Business Scope and Prioritisation of the Needs (MoSCoW)}
\label{sec:ch3-scope}

The objectives describe what the system should ideally do; delivering all of them at once, however, is
neither realistic nor wise for a first version. The scope was therefore prioritised with the
\textbf{MoSCoW} method, which sorts what is to be built into four levels --- \emph{Must have},
\emph{Should have}, \emph{Could have}, and \emph{Won't have (this time)} --- according to how essential
each element is to a useful first release (Table~\ref{tab:ch3-moscow}).

\paragraph{Two independent financial perimeters.}
Whichever axis is considered, one principle governs the whole scope: the costs and revenues of the two
services are never mixed. Dedicated transport and parcel delivery are each read on their own financial
perimeter, so that the profitability of one is never inflated or masked by the other. This separation
holds across every view of the system and is a direct response to the \emph{blurred financial
perimeters} identified in the diagnosis.
