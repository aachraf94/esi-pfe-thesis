\uchapter{General Conclusion and Perspectives}

This thesis set out from a clear problem: at Yalidine, as in much of the logistics sector, the
information needed to steer the business was not missing but \emph{scattered} --- dispersed across
several disconnected systems, slow to reconcile, and difficult to trust. The question that drove the work
was whether a unified, automated, and scalable Business Intelligence architecture could turn that
fragmented data into a reliable foundation for proactive, data-driven decisions across operations, cost
and profitability, and performance.

The answer is \textbf{LOGIQ} --- the decision-support system designed, built, and validated over the
preceding chapters. Part~I established the conceptual and technological foundations, from
decision-making and information systems to Business Intelligence and the logistics domain. Part~II
carried the system from a diagnosis of the existing situation (Chapter~3), through its conceptual design
(Chapter~4) and technical realisation (Chapter~5), to an end-to-end validation against a faithful,
production-scale test environment (Chapter~6). The result is an operational platform that consolidates
the company's scattered data, refreshes it automatically, surfaces it through decision-oriented
dashboards, and raises alerts the moment a monitored indicator drifts.

\section*{Summary of Contributions}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{Summary of Contributions}

Measured against the four objectives set at the outset, the work delivers on each: a \textbf{centralised
data warehouse} consolidating the heterogeneous sources, a \textbf{reliable automated ETL} that keeps it
current, \textbf{interactive dashboards} that turn the data into decision-oriented analytics, and a
\textbf{proactive alerting mechanism} that catches deviations early. Both prioritised business axes ---
\textbf{on-demand dedicated transport} and \textbf{parcel delivery} --- are delivered in full, each read
along the same three aspects (operations, cost and profitability, performance) and kept on its own
\textbf{independent financial perimeter} so that costs and revenues are never mixed.

Beyond meeting these objectives, the project makes a set of concrete engineering contributions
(Table~\ref{tab:concl-contributions}).

\section*{Limitations}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{Limitations}

The work is honest about the boundaries within which these results hold:

\begin{itemize}[noitemsep]
    \item \textbf{Simulated, not live, sources.} The production systems could not be connected within the
          scope of this project, so the validation rests on the simulation. Its fidelity to the source
          contract makes it a sound substitute, but the platform has yet to run against the real feeds.
    \item \textbf{Performance assessed qualitatively.} The aggregate layer and incremental load behave as
          designed under production-scale data, but the work reports no formal benchmark (response-time
          percentiles, throughput, refresh duration).
    \item \textbf{No live user-acceptance round.} The platform was validated technically; it has not yet
          been evaluated formally with the decision-makers in their day-to-day use.
    \item \textbf{Scope deliberately bounded.} Of the two prioritised axes, both are delivered; a third
          analytical capability --- route analysis --- was kept out of this version because the data it
          needs was not available within the project's scope.
\end{itemize}

None of these undermines the core outcome; each marks, instead, a clear and natural next step.

\section*{Future Work and Perspectives}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{Future Work and Perspectives}

LOGIQ was designed to evolve, and its architecture leaves room to grow without being reworked. Two
directions stand out, alongside a small number of consolidating steps (Table~\ref{tab:concl-future}).

\textbf{Route analysis.} The natural extension of the transport axis is to analyse \emph{how} goods move,
not only how much they cost. Once the routing and optimisation-reference data becomes available, this
capability can be added on top of the warehouse, which was already designed to accommodate it: a
route-optimisation component would compare the routes actually taken against optimised alternatives,
surfacing the gap as interactive maps and comparative KPI tables. It is deferred purely for want of the
input data, not for any architectural reason.

\textbf{Conversational analytics assistant.} The second perspective makes the platform reachable in plain
language. A decision-maker would express an information need in natural language --- in words --- and a
conversational assistant would translate it into the corresponding query over the warehouse and return
the matching visualisation: a chart, a map, or a KPI, generated on demand. This lowers the barrier to the
data still further, letting non-technical managers obtain a tailored view without navigating the
dashboards by hand.

Beyond these, the immediate consolidating steps are to point the ETL at the live production systems
(a change of configuration, not of design), to benchmark performance formally under load, and to run a
structured user-acceptance round. Further out, the existing facts offer a foundation for predictive
analytics --- forecasting volumes, costs, and performance --- extending LOGIQ from describing the past to
anticipating the future.

\section*{Closing Word}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{Closing Word}

This project began with fragmented data and a business unable to read its own activity with confidence.
It ends with a working, validated decision-support system that consolidates that data, keeps it current,
makes it visible along the dimensions that matter, and turns trustworthy figures into the basis for
proactive decisions. In doing so, LOGIQ answers the question that opened this thesis --- and, true to its
name, helps move the business from decisions taken on intuition to decisions grounded in logic and
evidence.
