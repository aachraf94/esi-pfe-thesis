\section*{Introduction}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{Introduction}

Every organisation runs on the decisions it makes. Yet the volume of data generated daily now far
exceeds what any manager can interpret unaided, and the quality of a decision increasingly depends on
the systems that turn that data into insight. This chapter builds the conceptual foundation of the
thesis along a single arc --- from the \textbf{abstract} to the \textbf{technical}.

\begin{itemize}[noitemsep]
    \item \textbf{Decision-making in organisations} (\S\ref{sec:ch1-decisions}) --- what a decision is,
          how decisions differ, and why unaided judgement is insufficient at scale.
    \item \textbf{From data to decisions} (\S\ref{sec:ch1-is}) --- how data becomes insight (the DIKW
          hierarchy), and the information systems, including Decision Support Systems, that deliver it.
    \item \textbf{Business Intelligence} (\S\ref{sec:ch1-bi}) --- the modern paradigm that operationalises
          data-driven decision support.
    \item \textbf{The building blocks of a BI system} (\S\ref{sec:ch1-bi-blocks}) --- the technical
          components (data warehouse, ETL, OLAP, dashboards, alerting) that Part~II designs and builds.
\end{itemize}

\section{Decision-Making in Organisations}
\label{sec:ch1-decisions}

Before any tool can support a decision, we must be clear about what a decision is, how decisions
differ from one another, and where they sit in an organisation. This section characterises decisions
and establishes why structured, data-driven support is needed.

\subsection{The Nature of a Decision}

A decision is the selection of a course of action from a set of alternatives in pursuit of an
objective. \textcite{simon1947} reframed it not as an instantaneous act but as a \emph{process}, and
his enduring model decomposes that process into three phases --- \textbf{intelligence} (recognising the
problem or opportunity), \textbf{design} (developing and analysing alternatives), and \textbf{choice}
(selecting one) --- to which practice adds \textbf{implementation} and review \parencite{simon1960}.

In the same spirit, \textcite{abbas2024bi} describes the operational decision cycle as five steps:
identifying the problem or opportunity, gathering the relevant data, defining and evaluating
alternatives, making the decision, and monitoring its outcome. The two views coincide: gathering and
evaluating data are exactly the activities a Business Intelligence system is built to support
(Figure~\ref{fig:ch1-decision-process}).

\subsection{Types of Decisions}

Decisions are not homogeneous. \textcite{simon1960} drew the foundational distinction between
\textbf{programmed} decisions --- repetitive and routine, reducible to rules or algorithms --- and
\textbf{non-programmed} decisions --- novel, consequential, and resistant to any fixed procedure. This
maps onto the now-standard spectrum of \emph{structured}, \emph{semi-structured}, and
\emph{unstructured} decisions.

The distinction is not merely taxonomic: it dictates the kind of support that is appropriate. As
\textcite{chalal2024} notes, structured decisions are best served by \emph{automation and information
systems}; semi-structured decisions call for \emph{analysis and simulation tools} (SWOT, PESTEL,
decision trees); and unstructured decisions rely on \emph{collaboration and creativity} (shared
workspaces, brainstorming, knowledge management). Table~\ref{tab:ch1-decision-types} summarises the two
poles of this spectrum.

\subsection{Bounded Rationality and the Need for Decision Support}

Why can these information needs not simply be met by managers themselves? Because human cognition is
limited. Simon's theory of \textbf{bounded rationality} holds that decision-makers face three
irreducible constraints --- incomplete information, finite processing capacity, and scarce time --- and
therefore \emph{satisfice}, accepting the first adequate option rather than the optimal one
\parencite{simon1957}.

These limits become acute precisely where this thesis operates. In a multi-site logistics network,
thousands of parcels are simultaneously in transit, profitability signals are buried in disconnected
systems, and performance deviations at one site silently propagate downstream. No manager can hold this
state in mind. The conclusion is direct: effective decision-making at scale requires structured
\textbf{decision support} --- systems that extend cognitive capacity, integrate scattered data, and
present results in a usable form. The remainder of the chapter examines how such systems are built.

\section{From Data to Decisions: Information Systems}
\label{sec:ch1-is}

Turning raw data into a decision is a progression. This section follows it in three steps: the
conceptual ladder from data to knowledge (DIKW), the information systems that institutionalise it, and
the Decision Support Systems that target managerial decisions directly.

\subsection{Data, Information, Knowledge}

Not all content carries the same decision value. The \textbf{DIKW hierarchy}, originating with
\textcite{ackoff1989} and later consolidated by \textcite{rowley2007}, distinguishes four ascending
levels, each adding context and usefulness to the one below:

\begin{itemize}[noitemsep]
    \item \textbf{Data} --- raw, unprocessed facts and figures (\eg a delivery cost of 1{,}200 DZD).
    \item \textbf{Information} --- data placed in context, answering \emph{who, what, where, when}
          (\eg the average delivery cost per zone this month).
    \item \textbf{Knowledge} --- patterns and rules derived from information, answering \emph{how}
          (\eg which routes systematically erode margin).
    \item \textbf{Wisdom} --- judgement that applies knowledge to act, answering \emph{why} and
          \emph{what to do} (\eg whether to re-price or re-route a service).
\end{itemize}

A BI system is, in essence, a machine for climbing this hierarchy (Figure~\ref{fig:ch1-dikw}): it
ingests data and lifts it, stage by stage, toward the knowledge on which decisions rest.

conventionally classified by the decision level they serve \parencite{laudon2022,gorry1971}, as

\subsection{Decision Support Systems}

Of these categories, the Decision Support System is the direct ancestor of Business Intelligence. A DSS
is ``an integrated set of software, databases, models and analysis tools designed to support
decision-makers in their decision-making process'', enabling users to pose complex questions over data
drawn from operational systems such as ERP, WMS, and CRM \parencite{abbas2024bi}. The concept was
formalised by \textcite{keen1978}, who stressed \emph{interactivity}: unlike batch reporting, a DSS
lets the user explore, adjust parameters, and receive immediate feedback.

As the field matured, \textcite{power2002} classified DSS by their primary enabling resource ---
\emph{data-driven} (the category relevant to this work), \emph{model-driven}, \emph{knowledge-driven},
\emph{document-driven}, and \emph{communication-driven}. Classical DSS were nonetheless limited by
their departmental scope, technical inaccessibility, and fragmented, manually updated data
\parencite{sharda2020}. Overcoming these limitations --- through an integrated, accessible,
continuously fed analytical foundation --- is exactly the ambition that gave rise to Business
Intelligence.

\section{Business Intelligence}
\label{sec:ch1-bi}

Business Intelligence (BI) is the modern, technical realisation of everything established above: the
discipline that turns an organisation's scattered data into a coherent, accessible basis for decisions.

\subsection{Definition and Strategic Value}

BI is ``a field of IT that includes the technologies, processes and applications used to collect,
manage and analyse data in order to support decision-making and optimise organisational performance''
\parencite{abbas2024bi}. \textcite{chaudhuri2011} define it as ``a collection of decision support
technologies for the enterprise, aimed at enabling knowledge workers to make better and faster
decisions'', while \textcite{negash2004} stresses the transformation of raw data into meaningful,
usable information. Three characteristics distinguish BI from earlier decision support:

\begin{itemize}[noitemsep]
    \item \textbf{A single, integrated data foundation} --- one authoritative repository (the data
          warehouse) that eliminates the conflicting departmental versions of the truth that plagued
          classical DSS.
    \item \textbf{An analytical orientation} --- BI reveals not only \emph{what} happened but
          \emph{why}, and increasingly \emph{what is likely next}.
    \item \textbf{Broad organisational accessibility} --- self-service dashboards extend analytics from
          a specialist elite to managers across the organisation \parencite{sharda2020}.
\end{itemize}

This value is not automatic. \textcite{yeoh2010} show that BI success depends on organisational
conditions --- executive sponsorship, clear business objectives, data quality and governance, and a
scalable architecture --- as much as on technology, a theme that recurs in Part~II.

\subsection{The BI Value Chain}

The transformation from raw data to insight follows a structured pipeline, the \textbf{BI value chain}
\parencite{chaudhuri2011}: heterogeneous \emph{sources} are consolidated by \emph{ETL} into a
\emph{data warehouse}, made navigable by \emph{OLAP and analytical} tools, and finally surfaced through
a \emph{presentation} layer of dashboards and alerts (Figure~\ref{fig:ch1-bi-valuechain}). With each
stage, raw records gain context and interpretive meaning, mirroring the DIKW ascent.

\section{The Building Blocks of a BI System}
\label{sec:ch1-bi-blocks}

This section names the technical components that make up a BI system. Each is introduced only at the
conceptual level here; their concrete design for this project belongs to Part~II
(Table~\ref{tab:ch1-building-blocks} maps each block to its role and its relevance to this work).

\subsection{The Data Warehouse}

The data warehouse is the integrated, authoritative store at the centre of a BI system ---
\textcite{inmon2005} defines it as a \emph{subject-oriented, integrated, time-variant and non-volatile}
collection of data supporting management decisions. Two design philosophies dominate: Inmon's
\emph{top-down} approach builds a normalised enterprise warehouse first, while Kimball's
\emph{bottom-up} approach builds \emph{dimensional} data marts and integrates them through conformed
dimensions \parencite{kimball2013}.

Dimensional models organise data into \textbf{fact} tables (the measurable events --- \eg a delivery
with its cost, distance, and margin) and \textbf{dimension} tables (the descriptive context ---
\emph{when, where, who}). Arranged around a central fact table, they form the canonical \emph{star
schema} of Figure~\ref{fig:ch1-star-schema}; deeper warehouse design is treated in Part~II.

\subsection{ETL --- Extract, Transform, Load}

The warehouse is populated by an \textbf{ETL} process: \emph{extracting} data from source systems,
\emph{transforming} it (cleansing, standardising, deduplicating, computing derived measures), and
\emph{loading} it into the warehouse \parencite{vassiliadis2009}. Transformation is where most of the
engineering effort and analytical value reside. Data quality is decisive: in an analytical setting,
errors compound through aggregation, so a single miscoded cost can contaminate every KPI that draws on
it \parencite{olson2003}.

\subsection{OLAP and Multidimensional Analysis}

Online Analytical Processing (OLAP), codified by \textcite{codd1993}, makes warehouse data navigable by
treating it as a multidimensional \emph{cube} whose axes are business dimensions such as time,
geography, and service category (Figure~\ref{fig:ch1-olap-cube}). The standard OLAP operations ---
\emph{roll-up} and \emph{drill-down} (changing granularity), \emph{slice} and \emph{dice} (selecting
subsets), and \emph{pivot} (reorienting the view) --- give analysts a vocabulary for exploring data
without writing complex queries \parencite{han2011}.

\subsection{Dashboards and Reporting}

The presentation layer is where analytical power becomes managerial value --- or fails to. Drawing on
\textcite{few2006}, an effective dashboard presents, on a single view, the information needed for a
specific decision, arranged for immediate comprehension: chart types matched to the question, colour
used to encode meaning rather than decoration, and a consistent visual grammar across views. A poorly
designed interface can render an otherwise excellent system useless.

\subsection{KPIs and Proactive Alerting}

A \textbf{Key Performance Indicator} (KPI) is a metric that quantifies progress against an objective
(\eg on-time delivery rate, cost per kilometre, gross margin). The most mature BI moves from
\emph{descriptive} reporting --- where the user must seek out a problem --- to \emph{proactive}
monitoring, where the system continuously evaluates KPIs against thresholds and notifies the
responsible party automatically when a breach occurs \parencite{sharda2020}. This proactive alerting,
which closes the loop between data and action, is a core deliverable of the platform and is designed in
detail in Part~II.

\section*{Conclusion}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{Conclusion}

This chapter traced a single arc: from the nature of a decision and the cognitive limits that make
unaided judgement insufficient, through the conceptual ladder from data to knowledge and the
information systems that climb it, to Business Intelligence as the modern paradigm of data-driven
decision support and the technical building blocks that compose it --- the data warehouse, ETL, OLAP,
dashboards, and proactive alerting.

These building blocks are general; their value depends on the domain in which they are applied.
