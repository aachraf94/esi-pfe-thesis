\chapter{Logistics in the Data Age}

\section*{Introduction}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{Introduction}

Chapter~1 established Business Intelligence as the technical means of data-driven decision support.
This chapter grounds that capability in the domain the platform serves: \textbf{logistics}. Logistics
is a vast, data-rich, and thin-margin domain in which decisions must be made continuously and under
pressure --- which makes it a natural setting for Business Intelligence. The chapter proceeds from the
general to the specific. It opens with \textbf{the logistics landscape} (\S\ref{sec:ch2-landscape}) ---
what logistics is, its strategic weight, and the data-age pressures reshaping it --- then examines
\textbf{how logistics operates} (\S\ref{sec:ch2-operates}) through the two complementary operating
models by which goods actually move. It turns next to \textbf{the economics of logistics}
(\S\ref{sec:ch2-economics}) --- cost, profitability, and performance, the three lenses through which any
logistics activity is read --- and closes with \textbf{Business Intelligence in logistics}
(\S\ref{sec:ch2-bi}), setting out why the domain suits BI and the integration gap this thesis sets out
to close.

\section{The Logistics Landscape}
\label{sec:ch2-landscape}

Logistics is the part of supply-chain management that plans, implements, and controls the movement and
storage of goods from point of origin to point of consumption \parencite{christopher2016}. More than
the physical movement of goods, it is the coordinated management of \textbf{three parallel flows} that
run through every supply chain (Figure~\ref{fig:ch2-three-flows}): a \emph{physical} flow of goods
downstream, an \emph{informational} flow of orders and tracking data, and a \emph{financial} flow of
payments \parencite{chopra2019}.

Operationally, logistics rests on a small set of core functions: \textbf{transport}, \textbf{warehousing
and handling}, \textbf{distribution}, and the final \textbf{last-mile} delivery to the customer. Far
from a back-office cost centre, logistics is a recognised source of competitive advantage: it accounts
for a substantial share of product cost, and superior logistics translates directly into service
quality and margin \parencite{christopher2016}.

\paragraph{The data-age pressures.}
Three forces have turned logistics into a data-intensive discipline. The first is \textbf{the e-commerce
surge}: explosive growth in parcel volumes, especially in emerging markets, raises both scale and
customer expectations for speed and real-time visibility \parencite{unctad2025, statista2025}. The
second is \textbf{digitalisation (Industry 4.0)}, as connected vehicles, IoT tracking, and warehouse
automation generate continuous streams of operational data \parencite{hofmann2017}. The third is
\textbf{the resulting data deluge}: the volume of data now generated across supply chains far outstrips
the capacity to exploit it, and that data remains scattered across poorly-integrated systems
\parencite{kpmg2024, logisticsviewpoints2026}.

\noindent These pressures set up the central tension of the domain: logistics generates more data than
ever, yet struggles to turn it into decisions --- the very gap Business Intelligence addresses.

\section{How Logistics Operates}
\label{sec:ch2-operates}

Goods move through two complementary \textbf{operating models} that differ in granularity, network
shape, and economics. A modern logistics operator typically runs both, on shared infrastructure but
with very different cost and service profiles.

\subsection{Networked Parcel Distribution and the Last Mile}

The first model handles a high volume of small, individual shipments through a \textbf{hub-and-spoke}
network: parcels are consolidated at hubs, line-hauled between regions, then fanned out for
\textbf{last-mile} delivery --- the final, most expensive and least predictable leg to the recipient's
door \parencite{christopher2016}. Each parcel follows a lifecycle of discrete states, from creation to
final resolution (Figure~\ref{fig:ch2-parcel-lifecycle}).

This model is monitored through a characteristic set of operational indicators: the
\textbf{delivery success rate}, \textbf{delays}, the \textbf{number of attempts} per parcel, and the
\textbf{return rate}. Because volumes are large and margins per parcel are thin, small percentage
movements in these indicators have a significant aggregate effect.

\subsection{Dedicated and On-Demand Freight Transport}

The second model is \textbf{dedicated} (business-to-business) transport: rather than sharing a parcel
network, a customer commissions dedicated capacity for a specific need. It is lower in volume but higher
in value per movement, and its economics are driven by vehicles, distance, and time rather than by
per-parcel handling \parencite{chopra2019}. Several on-demand service models coexist
(Table~\ref{tab:ch2-transport-services}), each with distinct cost drivers.

\section{The Economics of Logistics}
\label{sec:ch2-economics}

Logistics is a low-margin business in which decisions are won or lost on cost and service. This section
sets out the cost structure, the difficulty of seeing profitability, and the three analytical lenses
through which every logistics activity is read.

\paragraph{Cost structure.}
Logistics cost is distributed across transport, fuel, labour, handling, warehousing, and the last mile,
with transport and the last mile typically dominating (Figure~\ref{fig:ch2-cost-structure})
\parencite{christopher2016, chopra2019}. The relevant managerial concept is \textbf{cost-to-serve}: the
true, fully-loaded cost of serving a given shipment, route, or customer --- which often differs
markedly from the average and is invisible without integrated data.

\paragraph{Profitability and performance.}
Margin must be tracked per shipment and per trip, yet profitability deviations are hard to detect
inside raw operational systems, where the figures needed to compute them are dispersed. Alongside cost,
\textbf{service performance} --- on-time delivery, reliability, and adherence to service-level
agreements (SLAs) --- determines both customer retention and the penalties a provider incurs.

\paragraph{Three analytical lenses.}
Any logistics activity, in either operating model, is therefore read through the same three
complementary lenses --- \textbf{operations}, \textbf{cost \& profitability}, and \textbf{performance}
(Table~\ref{tab:ch2-lenses}). Together they give a complete picture of an activity rather than a purely
financial one; pricing, by contrast, is treated here only as a general cost concept, not as a
monitoring objective of this work.

\section{Business Intelligence in Logistics}
\label{sec:ch2-bi}

The picture built so far --- a high-volume, geographically dispersed, thin-margin domain, generating
streams of data across many systems --- describes an almost ideal setting for Business Intelligence.

\paragraph{Why logistics suits BI.}
Logistics combines every property that makes BI valuable: \textbf{high volume and velocity} of events,
wide \textbf{geographic spread}, \textbf{many heterogeneous source systems}, \textbf{thin margins} that
reward small efficiency gains, and \textbf{time-critical decisions} \parencite{wang2016}. In such a
domain, analytics deliver KPI monitoring, cost and profitability analysis, network and flow visibility,
and proactive alerting --- and they enable the climb from descriptive reporting toward predictive
capability discussed in Chapter~1 \parencite{sharda2020, hofmann2017}.

\paragraph{The integration gap.}
The obstacle is not a lack of data but its fragmentation. Operational, financial, and performance data
sit in separate, poorly-connected systems, so a unified, decision-oriented view --- across \emph{both}
operating models and all three analytical lenses --- rarely exists
(Figure~\ref{fig:ch2-integration-gap}) \parencite{enveyo2025, logisticsviewpoints2026}. Decision-makers
are left to reconcile fragments manually, slowly, and late.

\section*{Conclusion}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{Conclusion}

This chapter situated logistics as a data-rich, thin-margin, decision-intensive domain. It is built on
three flows and a handful of core functions; it operates through two complementary models --- networked
parcel distribution and dedicated transport --- whose distinct economics demand separate analysis; and
every activity within it is read through the lenses of operations, cost \& profitability, and
performance. The data-age pressures reshaping the sector have made it generate more data than ever,
while leaving that data fragmented across disconnected systems.

That fragmentation is the opening. A domain this data-rich and this margin-sensitive, yet this poorly
integrated, is precisely where Business Intelligence delivers its greatest value. Part~II takes up the
challenge directly, designing and building the platform that closes the integration gap for a
real, multi-site logistics operator.
